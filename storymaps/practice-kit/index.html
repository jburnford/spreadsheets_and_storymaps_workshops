Your course project is a public exhibit built in ArcGIS StoryMaps, a web tool for telling stories with maps, images, video, and text. This workshop gets you signed in, shows you how a story is put together, and gives you open-access materials to practise every block you are likely to use, before you start on your own project.

  • Time: about 90 minutes.
  • What you'll make: a practice story about Britain's industrial museums. It is not graded and is not part of your project.
  • Why it matters: the skills feed directly into your point map (Milestone 3), your chart (Milestone 4), and your final exhibit (Milestone 7).
  • Walkthrough video: coming soon.

Create a practice story

After you sign in you land on your Content page, where all your stories will be listed. Close any pop-up announcements with their ×. To get back here later, go to storymaps.arcgis.com/content.

Click + Create and choose Story. Ignore the other options (Briefing, Frame, Collection, Theme): your project is a Story.

Give your story a title, such as “Practice: Britain's industrial museums”. You can add a cover image now (try one from the Image section) or later.

Map: an express map

An express map is a simple map you draw yourself, right inside your story. Its advantage over a Map tour is that it isn't limited to points: you can also draw lines (routes, canals, railways) and areas (a coalfield, a district, a World Heritage Site). Start by adding a Map block (in the + menu under Data Visualization) and creating a new express map. The Edit express map window opens with these tools along the top:

  • Points: a plain point, or a numbered point (useful when the order matters).
  • Lines: a straight-segment line, or a freehand line.
  • Areas: a polygon, a freehand area, a circle, or a rectangle.
  • Text and arrows: labels and arrows to point things out.
  • Search by location (top right) to jump to a place by name.

Everything you draw appears in the Drawn features panel on the left, where you can give each feature a title, a description, and an image. The gear tab next to it holds the map settings, including the basemap. Click Save when you're done.

Try it:

  1. Search for each of the six museums below and add a numbered point for each, in the order of the Timeline. Give each point its name, description, and photo.
  2. Draw a line from Liverpool to Manchester for the Liverpool and Manchester Railway (1830).
  3. Draw an area around Blaenavon, where the ironworks and Big Pit sit within the Blaenavon Industrial Landscape World Heritage Site.
  4. Add a text label or an arrow, then try a different basemap in the settings.

Want more than an express map? If express maps feel too limited for your project, or you already have GIS experience (ArcMap or ArcGIS Pro), you can build a full web map in ArcGIS Online at spatialsk.maps.arcgis.com and add it to your story. Sidecar

Sidecar is often the best way to build a StoryMap. Each slide pairs a piece of text with a main media item (a map, image, video, or chart) that stays on screen while the reader works through the text. As the reader moves to the next slide, the main media changes: a different image, or the same map zoomed to a new place.

When you add a Sidecar, StoryMaps asks you to choose a layout. You can always change it later.

LayoutWhat readers seeGood for
DockedText and extra media scroll in a column beside the main media.Most stories: a map or image with a few paragraphs of explanation.
FloatingText scrolls in a card over the main media, which fills the background.Big, striking images or maps where you want the picture to dominate.
SlideshowText sits over the main media; readers click or tap an arrow to move to the next slide.Short, step-by-step sequences.

Try it: use the same six museums for both a Sidecar and a Map tour, so you can compare the two formats with identical content. Make a Docked sidecar with one slide per museum:

  • Main media: the museum's photo (in the Map tour section), or a map zoomed to the museum (coordinates in the Map section). Try both.
  • Text: the museum's name, its description, and a Button linking to its website.

Then switch the layout to Floating and then Slideshow to see how the same content changes.

Map tour

A Map tour takes readers from place to place, with an image and text at each stop. It is another strong option for a StoryMap, especially for the points you map in Milestone 3. When you add one, StoryMaps asks you to choose a layout:

Try it: build a Guided tour (Map focused) of the same six museums you used in your Sidecar, with the coordinates and descriptions from the Map section and the photos below. Put the stops in chronological order of what each site shows (the Timeline section will help). Then switch to an Explorer tour.

Compare: you now have the same six museums as a sidecar, a guided tour, and an explorer tour. Which do you like best? Which would suit the places and argument in your own project?
